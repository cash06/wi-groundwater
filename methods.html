How this model was made

The 3D model turns 1,664 Wisconsin municipal well records into three underground water surfaces, month by month from 2013 to May 2026. This page explains each step, from the raw readings to the picture, and how far each part can be trusted.

1The data

Every number in the model starts as a reading reported to the Wisconsin Department of Natural Resources by a city or village water utility. For each municipal well the DNR holds:

  • Static water level: depth to water after the pump has rested. This is the natural water level, and it is what the model is built from.
  • Pumping water level: depth to water while pumping, always deeper.
  • Water pumped each day, plus each well's construction report (depth, casing) and driller's log (the rock and sediment layers it passed through).

Locations are given only to the PLSS section, a one-mile-square survey block, so each well is placed at its section's centre. Land elevation comes from the USGS 10 m National Elevation Dataset, and the bedrock surface from the Wisconsin Geological and Natural History Survey (WGNHS).

2Cleaning the records

Raw records contain typing errors, sensor glitches, zeros and sudden jumps. Each well's static record goes through four steps:

  1. Remove zeros. A depth of exactly 0 ft would put water at the wellhead; in this data it means "no reading".
  2. Remove extreme values. Readings more than 2.5 standard deviations from the well's own average are dropped.
  3. Hampel filter, run twice. Each reading is compared with the median of the 15 readings around it. If it sits more than 2 scaled deviations away, it is a spike and is removed. If a well would lose more than half its readings this way, the filter refuses and the well is graded F instead.
  4. Fix level shifts. A detector looks for sudden jumps in the record and checks each against the pumping record: a jump that matches a real change in pumping is kept as genuine. One that doesn't is treated as an error (a re-surveyed reference point, a new sensor), and the record is shifted to close the gap, keeping its trends intact. 539 wells were flagged, and 363 were actually shifted.

Quality grades

Each well starts at 100 points and loses points for warning signs, then gets a letter grade (A 90+, B 80–89, C 70–79, D 60–69, F below 60). The grade is shown on every well in the model, and any grade can be switched off.

3Placing the wells

Position. 1,656 of 1,664 wells are located only to their section's centre, so wells in the same section share one point. On the map they are fanned out slightly so each can be seen and clicked, but the model uses the true section centre.

Aquifer band. Each well is sorted into one of three groundwater systems by where it takes in water: its open interval, from the bottom of the casing to the bottom of the well. That interval is compared with the depth to bedrock from the well's own driller's log.

Where a well's depth was missing from the DNR inventory, it was recovered from the construction report on the DNR's well-construction site.

4Building the surfaces

Each band's water surface is built on a grid of 2.5 km squares, using each well's median level over its whole cleaned record. Water levels mostly follow the land, so interpolating them directly between wells puts water above ground in valleys. The model works in two parts instead:

  1. Trend with the land. For each band, water-level elevation is fitted against land elevation with a straight line. Its slope b says how closely the water follows the land.
  2. Spread the leftovers. What the trend doesn't explain at each well (its residual) is spread across the grid by inverse-distance weighting: each square takes the 12 nearest wells of the same band, weighted by 1 / distance², and nothing is drawn more than 40 km from any well.

The slopes are a finding in themselves. Shallow (b = 1.015) and intermediate (b = 1.005) water tracks the land almost foot for foot, which is what an aquifer open to rain looks like. Deep water (b = 0.654) follows it far less, because it is sealed under confining rock and shaped by regional flow and pumping.

Bedrock is the WGNHS 100 m bedrock topography (Data Series 007), measured as depth below the land so it sits correctly under the terrain. Where bedrock is above the water table there is no sand-and-gravel aquifer, so the shallow layer is not drawn there.

5The time-lapse

For the timeline, each well's readings are reduced to one value per month: the median of its cleaned readings, so a well read daily doesn't count more than one read weekly. Each month the model then moves the median surface by how far each well sat above or below its own normal.

  • Set-aside months: a monthly value more than 50 ft from the well's normal is almost always an uncorrected error, so 3,727 such well-months are left out.
  • Persistence: groundwater changes slowly, so a well that skips a month keeps its last reading. Its weight fades by 6% for each month it carries, and it drops out after 12 months.

The model is monthly, not daily, because wells report on different days. A monthly step gives every well a value on the same footing, and groundwater rarely changes faster than that.

6The water budget

A water budget treats the landscape like a bank account:

P − ET − Q = ΔS

Precipitation (P) comes in; evapotranspiration (ET) and streamflow (Q) go out; what's left changes the water stored (ΔS) in snow, soil and groundwater. We don't measure P, ET or Q ourselves, so they come from the USGS Integrated Water Availability Assessment model (WRF-Hydro), monthly for each of the 1,807 sub-watersheds that touch Wisconsin. Watersheds on the border count only for the part inside the state.

On average Wisconsin gets 1,018 mm (40 in) of precipitation a year: about 61% returns to the air, 34% leaves in rivers, and +49 mm is left over.

The model's books don't balance

The water left over should equal the change in what the model stores. It comes out 58 mm a year too large, every year. The gap grows with lake surface: the model's precipitation, ET, snow and soil count land only, so water that runs into a lake and evaporates there drops out of its books. It's a bookkeeping gap, not missing water.

Where our wells come in

Everything above is the model. Our wells are real measurements, so they test it. For each water year we compare how much each shallow well rose or fell from one September to the next with the water the model says was left for the ground: P − ET − Q, minus what the soil and snow kept.

Shallow wells follow the budget; deep wells don't. Only the shallow aquifer is recharged directly by each year's weather, while the deeper ones respond more to pumping.

Specific yield ≈ 0.13. The slope says each foot the water table rose matched about 40 mm of water. A foot is 304.8 mm, so about 13% of the shallow aquifer is open space that fills with water. That is within the normal range for sand and gravel (0.1–0.25). It is a first estimate: 8 water years, model inputs, and pumped wells.

7Limitations

  • Positions are approximate. Section centres can be up to about 1 km from the real well, so read the surfaces as regional, not site-specific.
  • These are pumping wells. Municipal wells are pumped daily, so even "static" levels carry some pumping influence, most strongly in the deep aquifer.
  • Coverage is uneven. Surfaces are only drawn within 40 km of a well, and are least certain far from wells.
  • The water budget is modelled. P, ET and Q come from a model, not measurements, and overlap with our wells only for water years 2014–2021.
